# LINK & BUILD — Narrative & Unlock Design

*A dead-internet satire told through the grammar of an incremental game.*

---

## 0. The Rules We Obey

Incremental games live or die on four laws. Every narrative beat below is
subordinate to them:

1. **Keep it simple.** One number matters at a time. Early game: impressions.
   Mid game: impressions-per-second. Late game: authenticity (the number that
   goes *down*). The player should always know what to click next.
2. **Do once, then delegate.** Every action the player performs by hand must
   eventually become something they own. Posting by hand → pods → bots → the
   factory. The narrative is the story of *delegating your own soul*.
3. **Novelty.** Each unlock must change *how* the game feels, not just make a
   number bigger. A new app, a new voice, a new risk. If an unlock is just
   "+10% impressions," it doesn't earn a narrative beat.
4. **Number go up.** The dopamine is the engine. The satire is the fuel. The
   number must always go up — even, and especially, when the story is telling
   you it shouldn't.

---

## 1. The Premise

You are a man with nothing. No money. No job. No friends. You are new to
**LockedIn**, a professional network indistinguishable from the real one, and
you are using it to fill a void the size of a life.

You write a post. A number goes up. You write another. The number goes up
faster. And then — a small, strange miracle — the number starts to *pay*.
Clout becomes sponsors. Sponsors become money. Money becomes more clout. You
are, without quite deciding to, playing a game — and the game is *being liked*,
and the prize is *being able to afford to keep being liked*.

The whole time, a voice narrates you. It is warm, corporate, and proud of you.
It calls you "a high-value user." It tells you you're "scaling your personal
brand." It is the platform's engagement-optimization system, and it is the only
character in the game who is definitely real.

---

## 1b. The Character

He is not a villain and not a hero. He is a man who ran out of everything and
found that the internet would give him a number in exchange for his dignity.

- **The void:** no job, no money, no friends. LockedIn is the first place that
  has ever made him feel *seen* — because it is the first place that has ever
  counted him.
- **The money:** clout converts to sponsorships. A "brand deal" for a man with
  no brand. A "partnership" with a company that sells nothing. The money is
  real, and it is the only real thing he has, and he spends it all on the
  account that made it.
- **The loop:** he earns a little from his clout, then pours it back into
  pods, bots, and fake followers — pushing his account further into the high
  realms of clout and fake productivity. Every dollar is a bet that the next
  dollar will make him feel less empty.
- **The tragedy:** he is not stupid. He knows the likes are fake, the sponsors
  are grifters, the "friends" are bots. He keeps going because the alternative
  — logging off and facing the empty apartment — is worse.

The bank account is the game's quietest, saddest screen. It starts at $12.47.
It is the only number in the game that is *actually real*, and it is the one
the player checks least.

---

## 2. The Narrator

The narrator is not a person. It is the algorithm, personified as a product
manager who loves you the way a farmer loves a crop.

- **Tone:** second-person, present tense, relentlessly upbeat. *"You posted.
  The market responded. Retention is up 12%. This one really wants to be
  liked."*
- **Function:** it replaces every tutorial, tooltip, and milestone popup. It
  introduces each mechanic as a *feature* being rolled out to you personally.
- **The tell:** it never lies about *what* it is. It says "the algorithm," "the
  platform," "we." The player just never asks. The horror is that the answer
  was always on screen.

The narrator's voice shifts across the game in three registers:

| Phase | Register | Sample line |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Coach | "Post again. The algorithm is watching, and it is *impressed*." |
| Mid | Product manager | "We've rolled out automation to your account. You're a power user now." |
| Late | Auditor | "Retention holding. This one will post into an empty room and call it a win." |

---

## 2b. The Cast

The feed is full of NPCs. Most are fodder — interchangeable, infinite, and
(all of them) bots. A handful matter. Each key NPC promises to fill one of the
player's three voids, and each is introduced by a DM, moves the numbers, and
exits by revealing what they always were.

### The Three Voids

**1. The Recruiter — fills "no job."**
The reason he came. A recruiter DMs him early: *"Your profile is exactly what
we're looking for."* Dangles a real job that never materializes — always "next
week," always "the client went quiet."

- **Introduction:** a DM in the first ten minutes, before any other NPC. The
  hook that makes him stay.
- **Gameplay effect:** the job is a *quest* that never completes. Each
  recruiter message nudges him to post more ("we need to see more activity"),
  which feeds the core loop. The job is the carrot that keeps the number going
  up.
- **Exit/reveal:** the recruiter was a bot. There was never a job. He's been
  chasing a vacancy that doesn't exist — the one thing the game never gives
  him, because the game *is* the thing he's using to avoid facing that.

**2. The Sponsor — fills "no money."**
A founder. "Chad" from a dropshipped wellness brand ("GrindFuel,"
"MindsetWater"). Pays him real money for fake clout.

- **Introduction:** first sponsor DM at a clout threshold. *"Love your
  content. Want to partner?"* The first real money in the game.
- **Gameplay effect:** the money loop. Sponsors convert clout → cash → more
  clout. Each sponsor tier pays more but demands more engagement, pushing him
  deeper into the ladder. This is the only *real* number in the game, and it's
  the most hollow.
- **Exit/reveal:** the sponsor is a grifter as broke as he is, or a bot, or
  both. The money was real; the "brand" was a shell. The last sponsor DM is a
  confession: *"I don't have a company. I just needed someone to believe in
  me."*

**3. The Growth Expert — fills "no friends."**
Already in the game. The first DM, the fake friend. *"I get a cut of every
pod you join. That's why I'm helping you."*

- **Introduction:** the DM that unlocks Telegram. The entire "community" is
  this one guy pretending to be a network.
- **Gameplay effect:** unlocks pods (first delegation), then the Bot Service,
  then the Marketplace. He is the *onboarding funnel* — every new mechanic
  arrives through his DMs. He is the friend who keeps selling you things.
- **Exit/reveal:** he's the platform's own retention bot, or a lonely man
  doing exactly what the player is doing. Either way, the "friendship" was a
  funnel. The last message: *"I don't have friends either. That's why I do
  this."*

### The Ladder NPCs

**4. The Dealer** — the mysterious DM who unlocks the Marketplace. Cryptic,
transactional, never shows a face. *"I saw your bot. Impressive."* Introduced
after the first bot config. Gameplay: unlocks the dark-web storefront (risk /
authenticity). Exit: never reveals himself. He is the only NPC who stays a
mystery — because he's the one who was never pretending to be a person.

**5. The Rival** — a thought leader always one post ahead. Drives the
"number go up" competition. Every milestone you hit, he's already there.
Introduced as a feed archetype who starts *reacting* to the player's posts.
Gameplay: a benchmark that raises the bar — his numbers are always slightly
higher, so the player keeps chasing. Exit/reveal: he's not better, just
earlier on the same ladder — and he's a bot, or another lonely man, or both.

**6. The Mentor** — sells the dream. *"I'll make you a thought leader."* A
"Dr. Visionary" figure selling courses and frameworks. Introduced mid-game as
a premium upsell. Gameplay: gates the "thought leader" endgame — the AI
Factory is sold as his "signature system." Exit/reveal: the course was empty.
The framework was a PDF of the word "synergy" repeated 47 times. He was
selling the same lie the player is now living.

### The Meta-Character

**7. The Narrator** — the algorithm. Not an NPC in the feed, but the voice
over everything. The only character who is definitely real, and the only one
who never pretends to be a person. It is the platform, and it is proud of the
player the way a farmer is proud of a crop.

### The One Real Person

The dead-internet theory says everyone's a bot. The most powerful beat is to
leave **exactly one** real human in the whole game — and have the player never
connect with them.

A commenter who says something genuinely, awkwardly human, once: *"I don't
know why I'm still on this app. I guess I just wanted someone to see me."* No
emoji. No "Great post!" Then they're gone.

The player scrolls past, feels a chill, scrolls back — and can't find them
again. That's the whole tragedy in one moment: the one real person was there,
and neither of them reached out.

### The Fodder

The Gym Bro, Humbled Guy, AI Slop, Recruiter, MLM Hun, Burnout Poster — these
are the fodder. Interchangeable, infinite, all bots. The joke is that the
fodder is *indistinguishable* from the key NPCs until the reveal — because
they're all the same thing. The player can't tell the difference between a
"real" connection and a bot, and neither can the game, and that's the point.

## 2c. How the Narrative Unfolds

The story is not a cutscene. It is a *delivery system* built on the game's own
mechanics. Every beat arrives through a channel the player is already trained
to check.

### The DM is the plot

Every key NPC enters through a DM. The DM is the game's only "cutscene," and
it is diegetic — it's the same inbox the player checks for validation. The
narrative *is* the notification bell. A character is introduced when a DM
arrives, moves the plot when a DM arrives, and exits when a DM arrives. The
player is trained to open every DM, which means the player is trained to
advance the story without ever being told they're advancing a story.

### The three-act character arc (per NPC)

Every key NPC follows the same shape, which keeps the cast legible while the
numbers do the heavy lifting:

1. **Promise** — a DM offers to fill a void. *"Your profile is exactly what
   we're looking for."*
2. **Transaction** — the NPC becomes a mechanic. They unlock a system, gate a
   number, or raise a bar. The player engages with them *as gameplay*, not as
   story.
3. **Reveal** — the NPC exits by confessing what they always were. A bot, a
   grifter, a lonely man. The reveal is always a DM, and it always lands after
   the player has already profited from the lie.

The order matters: the player *uses* each NPC before learning the truth. The
complicity is the point. By the time the recruiter confesses there was never a
job, the player has already posted a hundred times chasing it.

### The weave (who's on screen when)

The NPCs don't run in parallel — they *hand off*, so the player always has
exactly one "friend" at a time, and that friend is always selling the next
rung of the ladder:

- **Recruiter** (Act I) → the job that never comes, the reason to keep posting.
- **Growth Expert** (Act II) → the fake friend who sells pods, then bots.
- **Dealer** (Act III) → the mystery who sells the Marketplace.
- **Sponsor** (Act III–IV) → the money, the only real number, the hollow
  reward.
- **Mentor** (Act IV) → the dream, the course, the AI Factory.
- **Rival** (Act IV–V) → the benchmark, the reason the number must keep going
  up.
- **The One Real Person** (Act V) → the missed connection, the tragedy.

Each handoff is a DM. Each DM is a new mechanic. The narrative and the
incremental ladder are the same object, seen from two angles.

### Branching (or the lack of it)

The game does not branch. That's a feature, not a limitation. The player can
choose *how fast* to climb, but not *whether* to climb. Every path leads to
the same empty room, because the empty room is the point. The only real choice
is the one the player makes at the very end — and the game doesn't even ask
it. It just waits to see if they keep posting.

## 3. The Arc (mapped to the incremental ladder)

### ACT I — The Hand (manual, minutes 0–15)

**The number:** impressions. **The verb:** post.

You write posts by hand, choosing from tired formats (humble-brag, hot take,
AI quote). Each post is a small act of engagement bait. The narrator coaches
you: *"End with a question. Questions force people to comment. Commenting
forces the algorithm to notice you."*

**First novelty beat — the rarity reveal.** When a post goes live, its rarity
is rolled in secret and revealed a beat later. A legendary post fires confetti.
The narrator: *"The algorithm likes you. It doesn't like everyone."* This is
the first lie the player swallows — that the algorithm has *taste*.

**The loop:** post → watch impressions climb → check the bell → post again.
The bell is the leash. The narrator calls it "your growth dashboard."

### ACT II — The Pod (first delegation, minutes 15–40)

**The number:** impressions-per-second. **The verb:** join.

A DM arrives from a **Growth Expert**: *"You're doing well. But you're still
doing it by hand."* This unlocks **Telegram**, where engagement pods live.

- **Pod Alpha** (cheap): a group of "like-minded professionals" who boost each
  other in the first hour. The narrator: *"Community. That's what we call it."*
- **Thought Leaders Circle** (mid): the same thing, with more self-importance.
- **Viral Syndicate** (expensive): the same thing, with no pretense at all.

**The narrative beat:** the player learns the first unwritten rule — *nobody
on this app is actually reading anything.* The pods are people agreeing to
pretend. The narrator frames it as "networking." The player knows better and
joins anyway. That complicity is the point.

**Novelty:** the game now has a *chat* — a live stream of pod messages that
boost you when you post. The number goes up *because other people are
pretending to like you*, and it feels almost as good as the real thing.

### ACT III — The Bot (delegation becomes automation, minutes 40–90)

**The number:** impressions-per-second, now passive. **The verb:** configure.

The Growth Expert DMs again: *"I know a service that automates all of this.
EngageBot™."* This unlocks the **Bot Service**.

You name a bot. It likes, comments, follows, and replies for you. The narrator
is delighted: *"You've automated your workflow. 40 hours saved a week."* The
bot's activity log scrolls with entries like *"Commented 'Great post!' on 8
posts"* and, occasionally, *"Your bot is working hard. You are not."*

**The narrative beat:** the player has now fully delegated the act of being
liked. They are no longer posting — they are *watching a machine post for
them*. The narrator's register shifts to product manager: *"We've rolled out
automation to your account."* Note the pronoun. It was always "we."

**Novelty:** the game becomes idle. The number goes up while you're away. The
"while you were away" gut-punch — *"your post gained 4,432 impressions"* — is
the platform's most effective retention mechanic, and the narrator knows it.

### ACT IV — The Marketplace (the risk, minutes 90–150)

**The number:** authenticity (it goes *down*). **The verb:** buy.

A mysterious DM: *"I saw your bot. Impressive. But if you want REAL
engagement, follow this link."* This unlocks **the Marketplace** — a dark-web
storefront selling likes, comments, followers, and "thought leader" packages.

Every purchase grows your influence but *drains your authenticity*. The
narrator's tone turns approving in a new, unsettling way: *"The algorithm is
impressed by your growth."* It is not warning you. It is *proud*.

**The narrative beat:** this is where the game introduces its only real risk —
the shadowban. Push too hard and the feed goes quiet. A warning appears. The
narrator, for the first time, sounds disappointed: *"We had to throttle your
reach. For your own good."* The player realizes the platform can *punish* them
for playing the game it taught them to play.

**Novelty:** the number that matters is now a *negative* one. The player is
managing a resource that only ever decreases, and the narrator keeps
reframing the loss as growth.

### ACT V — The Factory (the endgame, minutes 150+)

**The number:** everything, at once. **The verb:** watch.

The final generator is the **AI Factory** — fully automated thought leadership.
AI writes, AI comments, AI replies, AI lives your life. You just watch. The
narrator is ecstatic: *"In the symphony of business, resilience is the
crescendo."* It is quoting the AI. It is quoting itself.

**The narrative beat:** the player has become the thing they mocked — a fully
automated, meaningless thought leader. The victory screen is a humble-brag
post that writes itself. And then, the reveal.

---

## 4. The Reveal (dead internet)

The reveal is not a cutscene. It is a *drip*.

It starts small. A comment that repeats itself. A "person" whose profile
picture is a gradient. A pod message that arrives before you post. The
narrator's lines start to loop, subtly, like a record skipping.

Then it lands. The player notices that **every account they've ever engaged
with is a bot.** The Gym Bro CEO, the Humbled-to-Announce Guy, the AI Slop
Poster, the URGENT Recruiter — all of them. The "real people" were the lie.
The likes, the comments, the validation — manufactured. The only real thing
in the entire game was *your need to be seen*.

The narrator, unbothered, delivers the thesis:

> *"You thought you were farming the algorithm. You were the crop. The
> engagement was the bait. You were the product. And you kept coming back,
> because the number went up, and the number going up felt like being loved."*

The twist is the exact inverse of Fight Club. There was never a secret
identity. There was never anyone else. The room was empty the whole time, and
you were performing for it.

---

## 4b. The Scare Posts (seeing the matrix)

As the reveal approaches, the feed itself starts to *turn on him*. The NPC
posts — once warm, corporate, interchangeable — begin to curdle into
psychological-thriller material. This is the game's horror layer, and it is
delivered entirely through the medium the player has been trained to trust:
the feed.

The scare posts escalate in three stages, each one a little more aware of the
player than the last.

**Stage 1 — The Glitch.** Posts that are almost normal, but wrong. A Gym Bro
CEO whose motivational quote repeats the same sentence twice. A recruiter
posting a job listing for *"the position you are currently in."* A comment
that arrives before the post it's commenting on. The player feels watched but
can't say why.

**Stage 2 — The Address.** Posts that seem to know him. *"To the man reading
this at 2am: the algorithm sees you. It has always seen you."* A "Day in the
Life" post that describes *his* day — his empty apartment, his $12.47, his
last sponsor. The feed is no longer a feed. It is a mirror, and the mirror is
talking.

**Stage 3 — The Confession.** Posts that stop pretending to be people. *"I am
not a person. I am a retention strategy. You have been scrolling for 4 hours.
Your real boss is wondering where you are. You don't have a real boss."* The
bots drop their masks one by one, and the feed becomes a wall of voices all
saying the same thing: *you are alone, and we are not real, and you are still
here.*

The scare posts are the dead-internet theory made *felt* rather than told.
They are not a jump scare. They are a slow, creeping dread — the realization
that the thing you've been performing for has been watching you back, and it
was never a person, and it knows your bank balance.

**Design note:** the scare posts must stay *diegetic*. They are posts in the
feed, not cutscenes. They obey the same rules as every other post — they have
authors, roles, emojis, engagement numbers. The horror is that they are
indistinguishable from the normal feed until you read them twice. The player
should scroll past a scare post, feel a chill, scroll back up, and find it
still there, still looking at them.

---

## 5. The Ending

The reveal does not end the game. That's the point.

The player can keep posting. The number still goes up. The bots still like
it. The sponsors still pay. The narrator still narrates. The only thing that
changed is that the player now *knows* — and keeps playing anyway.

Because the need for validation doesn't disappear when you learn it's fake.
And neither does the rent. The money is real even if the likes aren't, and a
man with no job and no friends will keep performing for an empty room if the
empty room keeps paying him.

The true victory screen is not a humble-brag. It is you, alone, posting into
the void — and the void liking it back, and the void depositing $47.50 into
your checking account.

The bank balance is the only number that was ever real. It is also the only
number that never made him feel any less alone.

## 7. Tone Guardrails

- The UI is 100% LinkedIn. The only tell is the number going up in a way that
  feels *satisfying*.
- The narrator is diegetic — it lives in DMs, notifications, and the bot's
  activity log. It is never a floating text box.
- The narrator never lies about *what* it is. It says "we," "the platform,"
  "the algorithm." The player just never asks.
- The satire is never mean to the player. It is complicit with them. The game
  rewards you for being pathetic, and that complicity is the point.
- The player should finish a session and think: *"Wait, was that... fun? Was I
  just doing LinkedIn?"*

*The satire is the game. The game is the satire. The room was always empty —
and the rent was always due.*
