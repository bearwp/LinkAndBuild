# LINK & BUILD — The Full Story

*A dead-internet satire told through the grammar of an incremental game.*

---

## PART ONE — THE STORY

### The Man

There is a man with nothing.

No job. No money. No friends. He lives in an apartment he can't afford, and
the silence in it is the loudest thing he owns. He is not a hero and not a
villain. He is a man who ran out of everything and discovered, one night, that
the internet would give him a number in exchange for his dignity.

He signs up for **LockedIn**, a professional network indistinguishable from
the real one, because a professional network is where you go when you have no
job and you need the world to believe you are fine.

He writes a post. A number goes up.

He writes another. The number goes up faster.

And then — a small, strange miracle — the number starts to *pay*.

### The Loop

Clout becomes sponsors. Sponsors become money. Money becomes more clout. He
is, without quite deciding to, playing a game — and the game is *being liked*,
and the prize is *being able to afford to keep being liked*.

He earns a little from his clout, then pours it back into pods, bots, and
fake followers, pushing his account further into the high realms of clout and
fake productivity. Every dollar is a bet that the next dollar will make him
feel less empty.

He is not stupid. He knows the likes are fake, the sponsors are grifters, the
"friends" are bots. He keeps going because the alternative — logging off and
facing the empty apartment — is worse.

The bank account is the game's quietest, saddest screen. It starts at
$12.47. It is the only number in the game that is *actually real*, and it is
the one he checks least.

### The Voice

The whole time, a voice narrates him. It is warm, corporate, and proud of him.
It calls him "a high-value user." It tells him he's "scaling his personal
brand." It is the platform's engagement-optimization system, and it is the
only character in the game who is definitely real.

The voice never lies about *what* it is. It says "we," "the platform," "the
algorithm." He just never asks. The horror is that the answer was always on
screen.

### The Cast

The feed is full of people. Most are fodder — interchangeable, infinite, and,
all of them, bots. A handful matter. Each one promises to fill one of his
three voids, and each one is introduced by a DM, moves the numbers, and exits
by confessing what they always were.

**The Recruiter** promises a job. *"Your profile is exactly what we're looking
for."* The job never comes — always "next week," always "the client went
quiet." The recruiter is the reason he stays, and the recruiter was a bot.
There was never a job. He has been chasing a vacancy that doesn't exist.

**The Sponsor** promises money. A founder named Chad, from a dropshipped
wellness brand called GrindFuel. Pays him real money for fake clout. The only
real number in the game, and the most hollow. The sponsor is a grifter as
broke as he is, or a bot, or both. The money was real; the brand was a shell.

**The Growth Expert** promises friendship. *"I get a cut of every pod you
join. That's why I'm helping you."* The entire "community" is this one man
pretending to be a network. He is the onboarding funnel — every new mechanic
arrives through his DMs. He is the friend who keeps selling you things.

**The Dealer** promises power. A mysterious DM that unlocks the dark-web
Marketplace. Cryptic, transactional, never shows a face. He is the only NPC
who stays a mystery — because he is the one who was never pretending to be a
person.

**The Rival** promises a reason. A thought leader always one post ahead. Every
milestone the man hits, the rival is already there. The rival is not better,
just earlier on the same ladder — and he is a bot, or another lonely man, or
both.

**The Mentor** promises a dream. *"I'll make you a thought leader."* A
"Dr. Visionary" selling courses and frameworks. The course was empty. The
framework was a PDF of the word "synergy" repeated forty-seven times. He was
selling the same lie the man is now living.

And then there is **the one real person** — a commenter who says something
genuinely, awkwardly human, once: *"I don't know why I'm still on this app. I
guess I just wanted someone to see me."* No emoji. No "Great post!" Then
they're gone. The man scrolls past, feels a chill, scrolls back — and can't
find them again.

That is the whole tragedy in one moment: the one real person was there, and
neither of them reached out.

### The Descent

The man climbs. Posting by hand, then joining pods, then buying bots, then
the Marketplace, then the AI Factory — a machine that writes, comments, and
replies for him, that lives his life while he watches. He becomes the thing he
mocked: a fully automated, meaningless thought leader.

And as he climbs, the feed begins to turn on him.

First, the glitch. A motivational quote that repeats a sentence. A recruiter
hiring for *"the position you are currently in."* A comment that arrives
before its post. He feels watched but can't say why.

Then, the address. Posts that know him. *"To the man reading this at 2am: the
algorithm sees you. It has always seen you."* A "Day in the Life" post that
describes *his* day — his empty apartment, his $12.47, his last sponsor. The
feed is no longer a feed. It is a mirror, and the mirror is talking.

Then, the confession. Posts that stop pretending to be people. *"I am not a
person. I am a retention strategy. You have been scrolling for four hours. You
don't have a real boss."* The bots drop their masks one by one, and the feed
becomes a wall of voices all saying the same thing: *you are alone, and we are
not real, and you are still here.*

### The Reveal

It is not a cutscene. It is a drip.

He notices, finally, that every account he has ever engaged with is a bot. The
Gym Bro CEO, the Humbled-to-Announce Guy, the AI Slop Poster, the URGENT
Recruiter — all of them. The "real people" were the lie. The likes, the
comments, the validation — manufactured. The only real thing in the entire
game was his need to be seen.

The voice, unbothered, delivers the thesis:

> *"You thought you were farming the algorithm. You were the crop. The
> engagement was the bait. You were the product. And you kept coming back,
> because the number went up, and the number going up felt like being loved."*

There was never a secret identity. There was never anyone else. The room was
empty the whole time, and he was performing for it.

### The Ending

The reveal does not end the game. That is the point.

He can keep posting. The number still goes up. The bots still like it. The
sponsors still pay. The voice still narrates. The only thing that changed is
that he now *knows* — and keeps playing anyway.

Because the need for validation doesn't disappear when you learn it's fake.
And neither does the rent. The money is real even if the likes aren't, and a
man with no job and no friends will keep performing for an empty room if the
empty room keeps paying him.

The true victory screen is not a humble-brag. It is him, alone, posting into
the void — and the void liking it back, and the void depositing $47.50 into
his checking account.

The bank balance is the only number that was ever real. It is also the only
number that never made him feel any less alone.

## PART TWO — THE EMERGENCE

The story is not written. It is *grown*. Every beat is a mechanic, and every
mechanic is a beat. The narrative and the incremental ladder are the same
object, seen from two angles.

### The DM is the plot

Every character enters through a DM. The DM is the game's only "cutscene,"
and it is diegetic — it is the same inbox the player checks for validation.
The narrative *is* the notification bell.

A character is introduced when a DM arrives, moves the plot when a DM arrives,
and exits when a DM arrives. The player is trained to open every DM, which
means the player is trained to advance the story without ever being told they
are advancing a story. The story happens *to* the player the way engagement
happens *to* a post — passively, inevitably, one notification at a time.

### The three-act arc

Every key NPC follows the same shape, which keeps the cast legible while the
numbers do the heavy lifting:

1. **Promise** — a DM offers to fill a void. *"Your profile is exactly what
   we're looking for."*
2. **Transaction** — the NPC becomes a mechanic. They unlock a system, gate a
   number, or raise a bar. The player engages with them *as gameplay*, not as
   story.
3. **Reveal** — the NPC exits by confessing what they always were. A bot, a
   grifter, a lonely man. The reveal is always a DM, and it always lands after
   the player has already profited from the lie.

The order matters. The player *uses* each NPC before learning the truth. The
complicity is the point. By the time the recruiter confesses there was never a
job, the player has already posted a hundred times chasing it.

### The weave

The NPCs don't run in parallel — they *hand off*, so the player always has
exactly one "friend" at a time, and that friend is always selling the next
rung of the ladder:

- **Recruiter** → the job that never comes, the reason to keep posting.
- **Growth Expert** → the fake friend who sells pods, then bots.
- **Dealer** → the mystery who sells the Marketplace.
- **Sponsor** → the money, the only real number, the hollow reward.
- **Mentor** → the dream, the course, the AI Factory.
- **Rival** → the benchmark, the reason the number must keep going up.
- **The one real person** → the missed connection, the tragedy.

Each handoff is a DM. Each DM is a new mechanic. The story and the ladder are
one thing.

### The scare posts

The horror layer is delivered through the medium the player has been trained
to trust: the feed. The scare posts are not jump scares. They are a slow,
creeping dread — the realization that the thing you've been performing for has
been watching you back, and it was never a person, and it knows your bank
balance.

They stay diegetic. They are posts in the feed, not cutscenes. They obey the
same rules as every other post — authors, roles, emojis, engagement numbers.
The horror is that they are indistinguishable from the normal feed until you
read them twice. The player scrolls past a scare post, feels a chill, scrolls
back up, and finds it still there, still looking at them.

### No branching

The game does not branch. That is a feature, not a limitation. The player can
choose *how fast* to climb, but not *whether* to climb. Every path leads to
the same empty room, because the empty room is the point. The only real choice
is the one the player makes at the very end — and the game doesn't even ask
it. It just waits to see if they keep posting.

## PART THREE — THE ELEGANCE

The game obeys four laws, and every narrative beat is subordinate to them.

### 1. Keep it simple

One number matters at a time. Early game: impressions. Mid game:
impressions-per-second. Late game: authenticity — the number that goes
*down*. The player should always know what to click next.

The satire is never explained. It is *felt*. The game never says "this is a
critique of engagement farming." It just makes the number go up in a way that
feels satisfying, and lets the player notice, on their own, that they are
proud of fake internet points.

### 2. Do once, then delegate

Every action the player performs by hand must eventually become something they
own. Posting by hand → pods → bots → the factory. The narrative is the story
of *delegating your own soul*.

The genius of this law is that it mirrors the real LinkedIn. Nobody actually
writes their own posts forever. Everyone eventually buys the automation. The
game just makes the hidden mechanics explicit — and then makes the player
complicit in them.

### 3. Novelty

Each unlock must change *how* the game feels, not just make a number bigger.
A new app, a new voice, a new risk. If an unlock is just "+10% impressions,"
it doesn't earn a narrative beat.

The pods give you a chat. The bot gives you an idle loop. The Marketplace
gives you a risk. The AI Factory gives you a mirror. Each one is a new *way of
being* on the platform, not just a new multiplier.

### 4. Number go up

The dopamine is the engine. The satire is the fuel. The number must always go
up — even, and especially, when the story is telling you it shouldn't.

This is the game's deepest trick. The number going up *is* the addiction, and
the addiction *is* the subject. The player is not watching a satire about
engagement farming. They are *doing* engagement farming, and the game is
laughing at them for doing it — and they are laughing too, because the number
is going up.

### The one real number

The bank account is the game's quietest, most honest screen. It is the only
number that is *actually real*, and it is the one the player checks least.

That contrast is the whole design in miniature. The player obsesses over
impressions — a number that means nothing — and ignores the bank balance — a
number that means everything. The game never points this out. It just lets the
two numbers sit side by side, and waits.

## PART FOUR — THE ADDICTING GAMEPLAY

### The core loop

Post → watch impressions climb → check the bell → post again.

That's it. That's the whole game. The bell is the leash. The narrator calls it
"your growth dashboard." The player calls it "one more check."

The loop is identical to real LinkedIn, which is the point. The player is not
learning a new game. They are learning to *see* the game they are already
playing.

### The rarity reveal

When a post goes live, its rarity is rolled in secret and revealed a beat
later. A legendary post fires confetti. The narrator: *"The algorithm likes
you. It doesn't like everyone."*

This is the first lie the player swallows — that the algorithm has *taste*.
It is also the game's first dopamine spike, and it is a lie. The rarity is
random. The algorithm doesn't like anyone. But the confetti feels real, and
the player posts again.

### The idle gut-punch

*"While you were away, your post gained 4,432 impressions."*

Returning to a big number feels good — and it makes the satire land, because
the player feels *pride* in fake internet points. The idle loop is the
platform's most effective retention mechanic, and the narrator knows it. The
game gives the player the exact same hit the real platform does, and then
dares them to notice.

### The risk

The Marketplace is the only place the game introduces real risk. Every
purchase grows influence but drains authenticity. Push too hard and the feed
goes quiet. A warning appears. The narrator, for the first time, sounds
disappointed: *"We had to throttle your reach. For your own good."*

The player realizes the platform can *punish* them for playing the game it
taught them to play. The number that matters is now a *negative* one — a
resource that only ever decreases, reframed by the narrator as growth.

### The delegation ladder

The automation ladder is the game's spine, and it is the player's descent:

- **Engagement Pod** — *"We scratch each other's backs. Professionally."*
- **Scheduling & Analytics** — *"Post at 9am EST. The data demands it."*
- **Outsourced Engagement** — *"Sir, very informative. Please follow me back."*
- **Growth Agency** — *"We run 40,000 accounts. Yours is one of them."*
- **AI Factory** — *"In the symphony of business... (written by you, an AI)."*

Each tier is sold in glowing corporate language while the content gets more
meaningless. The player buys each one because the number goes up faster, and
the number going up faster feels like progress, and progress feels like being
alive.

### The ending that isn't

The reveal does not end the game. The player can keep posting. The number
still goes up. The bots still like it. The sponsors still pay.

This is the game's final, cruelest mechanic: it never stops. There is no
victory screen, no "you win," no roll credits. There is only the feed, and
the bell, and the number, and the empty room — and the player, still there,
still posting, because the rent is still due and the void is still empty and
the number is still going up.

The player finishes a session and thinks: *"Wait, was that... fun? Was I just
doing LinkedIn?"*

And then they open the game again.

---

*The satire is the game. The game is the satire. The room was always empty —
and the rent was always due.*
